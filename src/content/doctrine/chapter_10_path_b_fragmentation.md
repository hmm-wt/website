**The Necessity Doctrine, Chapter 10**
*2 May 2026*

---

Path B is the divergence scenario. The four markets do not converge. Each tightens or relaxes its regulatory architecture in response to domestic political and fiscal pressures, and the cross-border friction increases rather than decreases. Singapore moves toward a lighter touch to defend regional competitiveness. Japan tightens further to align with EU and U.S. allies. Australia muddles through with mixed signals. New Zealand retreats from the active R&D-funding posture under fiscal pressure.

This path is plausible. Each component has historical precedent. The doctrine treats it as a real scenario with a coherent portfolio response, not a tail risk to be ignored. The mechanism that produces returns under fragmentation is different from the mechanism under convergence. Specialisation replaces optionality.

> **What fragmentation looks like by 2028**
>
> - **Singapore:** retains regulatory sandbox lead but rolls back AI risk guidelines under industry pressure; positions as the light-touch outlier
> - **Japan:** tightens AI Promotion Act with phased penalties under v1.3 or v2.0 guidelines; aligns more explicitly with EU AI Act
> - **Australia:** retains APRA prudential discipline but loses RDTI scope under fiscal review; superannuation policy uncertainty raises LP friction
> - **New Zealand:** MBIE Innovation Services budget compressed; grant-funded R&D contracts; medtech and agritech remain regulated but the startup ecosystem weakens
> - **Cross-border:** sandbox bridges stall; medical device cross-recognition contracts; export-control regimes diverge on critical materials

## How fragmentation happens

The convergence trajectory described in chapter 9 depends on continued bilateral commitment from each of the four regulators and continued political stability domestically. Either condition can break.

Bilateral commitment can break under specific shocks. A high-profile cross-border financial crime case using a sandbox passport. A medical device approval failure that propagates through a recognition agreement. A geopolitical realignment that removes one party from a previously bilateral framework. Each is plausible inside a five-year window.

Domestic political stability can break for different reasons. A change of government that re-prioritises fiscal restraint over innovation policy. A high-profile corporate failure that triggers regulatory tightening or rollback. A sustained recession that shifts policy attention away from technology governance. Each has historical precedent in at least two of the four markets.

The fragmentation scenario does not require all four markets to fragment. It requires enough of them to fragment that the cross-border passport thesis breaks and each market reverts to a standalone regulatory architecture.

---

## What fragmentation does to T1 portfolio companies

A T1 company in fragmentation operates inside a single primary regulatory perimeter and faces real friction extending into adjacent markets. The licence in MAS does not produce expedited review in HSA. The TGA approval does not transfer to PMDA. The cross-border procurement reference loses cite-ability.

The implication for valuation is downward in the cross-border component and steady or upward in the standalone-licence component. A T1 company with a deeply held single-market licence is more valuable under fragmentation, not less, because the standalone barrier is the only barrier that holds. Companies whose business case depended on the cross-border thesis are mispriced under fragmentation.

The portfolio response is concentration in deep single-market T1 positions. A medtech with a strong domestic Japanese position and a Japanese strategic acquirer pathway holds value under fragmentation. A medtech whose plan was a four-market roll-out from a Singapore base does not.

---

## What fragmentation does to T2 portfolio companies

T2 vendors face longer sales cycles in cross-border deployments. The reference-deployment asset travels less efficiently. The compliance work becomes more market-specific, which raises the engineering and product cost of multi-market expansion.

The structural response for T2 is product modularity. A vendor that ships a configurable compliance platform that adapts to multiple regulatory regimes performs better under fragmentation than a vendor with one regime hard-coded. The investment thesis at seed under fragmentation prefers T2 companies with explicit multi-regime architecture rather than companies dependent on regime convergence.

The customer mix also shifts. T2 vendors under fragmentation have higher concentration in their primary market and more limited expansion into adjacent markets. The exit valuation multiple compresses for the same revenue level, because the strategic acquirer pays for the addressable market reach, which fragmentation has narrowed.

---

## Where the returns still come from

Fragmentation does not eliminate the regulatory-barrier mechanism. The barrier is statutory in each market individually. The licences are still hard to issue, the compliance is still expensive, and the procurement references still travel within the market that produced them. The mechanism is local rather than regional, which changes the economics but does not invalidate them.

Three return paths remain.

First, the absolute regulatory advantage in any one market. A T1 company with a deep Japanese position is still defended by its licence under fragmentation. The barrier is intra-market and holds.

Second, the friction premium across boundaries. Fragmentation creates cross-border compliance friction. Companies that solve the friction (translation services, multi-regime compliance platforms, cross-licensing intermediaries) earn the friction premium. The companies are different from the convergence-thesis companies. The underlying mechanism is fragmentation itself, not its absence.

Third, the absolute capital efficiency of regulated incumbency. Even in a fragmenting world, the cost of building a T1 entity from scratch is higher than the cost of acquiring one. Strategic acquirers buy regulated entities to compress time-to-market. The acquirer pool may narrow geographically, but the per-deal valuation in the relevant geography holds.

## The portfolio construction under path B

Different from path A. Concentrate on deep single-market T1 positions with strong domestic exit acquirer pools. Underweight the multi-licence optionality bet. Allocate explicitly to T2 vendors with multi-regime modular architecture. Reserve a position for fragmentation-thesis companies that solve cross-border friction. Anticipate longer holding periods on the cross-border thesis companies that survive into the convergence-or-fragmentation resolution.

Path B compresses the regional-portfolio thesis but does not eliminate it. The four-market construction still holds because each market has independent regulatory architecture that produces independent returns. The cross-market correlation structure is what changes.

---

## Comparing path A and path B

The doctrine does not assign probability weights between the two paths. Both are plausible. The observable evidence from 2024-2026 leans toward path A but does not exclude path B. The portfolio construction differs in emphasis, not in core thesis.

The shared elements: regulated-sector concentration, four-market diversification, T1/T2 binary classifier, 2024-2028 deployment window. The differing elements: cross-market optionality weighting, T2 architectural preference, exit acquirer mix.

A portfolio construction designed for path A but executed under path B underperforms but does not break. A portfolio construction designed for path B but executed under path A under-realises the option value but does not break. The construction that is robust to both is concentrated in the regulatory-barrier mechanism and indifferent to the convergence-or-fragmentation question over the deployment period.

---

## Closing position of this paper

The doctrine is testable. The conservative classifier is the headline empirical anchor: 33.5% of the 19,416 four-market universe (6,505 companies) passes the T1 / T2 screen. Everything else either rests on documented regulatory record or is flagged as inference. The four-market construction is specific enough to be wrong. The Five Necessities are specific enough to be reframed. The 2024-2028 window is dated enough to be falsified.

What it claims: regulatory architecture produces durable barriers, the four markets have built deeper architecture than the alternatives, the seed-stage entry into that architecture is mispriced, and the period for capturing the mispricing is closing.

What it does not claim: that other markets cannot produce returns. That other theses are wrong. That this thesis is the only correct one. The claim is narrower. Inside the regulated-sector seed thesis, executed in these four markets, during this window, the structural advantage is observable.
