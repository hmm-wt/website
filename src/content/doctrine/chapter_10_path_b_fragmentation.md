**The Necessity Doctrine, Chapter 10**
*2 May 2026*

---

Path B is the divergence scenario. The four markets do not converge. Each tightens or relaxes its regulatory architecture in response to domestic political and fiscal pressures. Cross-border friction increases.

Singapore moves toward a lighter touch to defend regional competitiveness. Japan tightens to align with EU and US allies. Australia muddles through with mixed signals. New Zealand retreats under fiscal pressure.

This path is plausible. Each component has historical precedent. The mechanism that produces returns under fragmentation is different from convergence. Specialisation replaces optionality.

> **What fragmentation looks like by 2028**
>
> - **Singapore:** retains sandbox lead but rolls back AI risk guidelines under industry pressure
> - **Japan:** tightens AI Promotion Act with phased penalties; aligns with EU AI Act
> - **Australia:** retains APRA discipline but loses RDTI scope under fiscal review
> - **New Zealand:** MBIE Innovation Services budget compressed; medtech and agritech regulated but startup ecosystem weakens
> - **Cross-border:** sandbox bridges stall; medical device cross-recognition contracts; export-control regimes diverge

---

## How fragmentation happens

The convergence trajectory depends on continued bilateral commitment and domestic political stability. Either can break.

Bilateral commitment breaks under shocks. A high-profile cross-border financial crime case using a sandbox passport. A medical device approval failure that propagates through a recognition agreement. A geopolitical realignment that removes one party.

Domestic stability breaks for different reasons. A change of government re-prioritising fiscal restraint. A high-profile corporate failure triggering regulatory tightening. A sustained recession that shifts policy attention. Each has precedent in at least two of the four markets.

---

## What fragmentation does to T1

A T1 company operates inside a single primary perimeter and faces real friction extending into adjacent markets. The licence in MAS does not produce expedited review in HSA. The TGA approval does not transfer to PMDA.

The implication: downward in the cross-border component, steady or upward in the standalone-licence component. A T1 company with a deeply held single-market licence is more valuable under fragmentation, not less, because the standalone barrier is the only barrier that holds.

A medtech with a strong domestic Japanese position and a Japanese strategic acquirer pathway holds value. A medtech whose plan was a four-market roll-out from a Singapore base does not.

---

## What fragmentation does to T2

T2 vendors face longer cross-border sales cycles. The reference-deployment asset travels less efficiently. The compliance work becomes market-specific, which raises engineering and product cost.

The structural response is product modularity. A vendor with a configurable compliance platform performs better than one with a regime hard-coded.

---

## Where the returns still come from

Fragmentation does not eliminate the regulatory-barrier mechanism. The barrier is statutory in each market individually. Licences are still hard to issue, compliance is still expensive, procurement references still travel within the market that produced them.

Three return paths remain.

1. **Absolute regulatory advantage** in any one market. A T1 with a deep Japanese position is defended by its licence.
2. **Friction premium** across boundaries. Companies that solve cross-border compliance (translation, multi-regime platforms, cross-licensing intermediaries) earn the premium.
3. **Capital efficiency of regulated incumbency.** The cost of building a T1 entity is higher than acquiring one. Strategic acquirers buy to compress time-to-market.

---

## Closing position

The doctrine is testable. 33.5% of the 19,416 four-market universe (6,505 companies) passes the T1 / T2 screen. Everything else rests on documented regulatory record or is flagged as inference. The four-market construction is specific enough to be wrong. The Five Necessities are specific enough to be reframed. The 2024–2028 window is dated enough to be falsified.

What it claims: regulatory architecture produces durable barriers. The four markets have built deeper architecture than the alternatives. The seed-stage entry into that architecture is mispriced. The period for capturing the mispricing is closing.

What it does not claim: that other markets cannot produce returns. That other theses are wrong. That this is the only correct one. The claim is narrower. Inside the regulated-sector seed thesis, executed in these four markets, during this window, the structural advantage is observable.<span class="pause" aria-label="pause"></span>
