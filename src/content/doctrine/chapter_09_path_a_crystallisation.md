**The Necessity Doctrine, Chapter 9**

---

Path A is the convergence scenario. Japan, Australia, New Zealand, and Singapore each continue building regulatory architecture through 2028 and the four architectures coordinate enough to produce a recognisable regional standard.

The mechanism is not a treaty. It is the cumulative effect of bilateral regulatory bridges, coordinated AI guidelines, harmonised medical device pathways, aligned export-control regimes. This is the trajectory implied by the observable record. It is also the path that produces the highest regulatory-asset value at exit, because a regulated company that has cleared one of the four perimeters gains optionality across the other three.

> **What convergence looks like by 2028**
>
> - **AI governance:** Japan METI, Singapore MAS, EU AI Act interoperable in practice. Multi-regime compliance with one build.
> - **Medical device approval:** TGA, Medsafe, HSA, PMDA harmonised on SaMD classification. Cross-recognition pathways operational.
> - **Financial services:** APRA, MAS, JFSA, RBNZ aligned on operational resilience. DORA-equivalent provisions adopted bilaterally.
> - **Critical materials:** four markets aligned with US and EU export-control regimes.
> - **Sandbox passporting:** active financial-services bridges between MAS, APRA, JFSA pilot regimes.

---

## How convergence happens without a treaty

Regulatory convergence in Asia-Pacific has historically failed when attempted through formal multilateral instruments. ASEAN financial integration moved slowly. APEC harmonisation was aspirational. The successful pattern is bilateral, sectoral, incremental.

The mechanism is recognition rather than harmonisation. Singapore's HSA recognises FDA approvals for expedited review. Australia's TGA accepts certain CE Marks. New Zealand's Medsafe defers to TGA decisions. Japan's PMDA has bilateral agreements with the FDA and EMA.

The financial-services parallel is the same. MAS-FCA, MAS-CSSF, and APRA-MAS bilateral bridges allow regulated entities in one jurisdiction to license in another with reduced friction.[^1] The bilateral count has roughly doubled since 2020. By 2028 the network is dense enough to constitute a regional regulatory passport.

---

## What convergence does to T1

A T1 company with a primary licence in one of the four markets gains a structural pathway into the other three. Not automatic. Still requires local counsel, capital, entity structuring. But the regulatory submission, compliance work, and policy posture transfer with reduced cost.

Seed valuations price on first-licence path. Convergence adds secondary licence optionality without changing the seed price. The arbitrage closes by Series B or C as the secondary submissions produce evidence.

---

## What convergence does to T2

A T2 vendor deployed inside one regulated buyer becomes a reference for buyers in adjacent markets faster than under the current regime. The procurement-reference asset travels.

A KYC vendor deployed inside an MAS-licensed bank is being purchased by APRA-regulated banks because the underlying frameworks are aligned. Same for clinical trial software across PMDA, TGA, and Medsafe sponsors.

---

## Capital and exit implications

North American strategic acquirers find pure-APAC targets more interesting when extending APAC operations is cheaper. EMEA strategic acquirers find regulatory-aligned APAC targets easier to integrate.

The IPO path widens. Cross-listing between ASX, SGX, TSE, and the US becomes more credible because the regulatory baseline is similar. Sovereign and pension capital allocates more readily to regional funds.

---

## The portfolio construction under path A

Concentrate on T1 with multi-licence optionality and T2 with cross-border procurement reference. Underweight pure-domestic plays. Hold longer than the seven-year venture standard. Reserve dry powder for follow-on into companies that demonstrate cross-market traction by Series A.
