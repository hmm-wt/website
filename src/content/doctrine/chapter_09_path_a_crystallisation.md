**The Necessity Doctrine, Chapter 9**
*2 May 2026*

---

Path A is the convergence scenario. Japan, Australia, New Zealand, and Singapore each continue building regulatory architecture through 2028 and beyond, and the four architectures coordinate enough to produce a recognisable regional standard. The mechanism is not a treaty. It is the cumulative effect of bilateral financial-services regulatory bridges, coordinated AI guidelines, harmonised medical device pathways, and aligned export-control regimes.

This path is the trajectory implied by the observable regulatory record, the publicly stated intentions of the four governments, and the cross-border instruments already in operation. It is also the path that produces the highest regulatory-asset value at exit, because a regulated company that has cleared one of the four perimeters gains optionality across the other three.

> **What convergence looks like by 2028**
>
> - **AI governance:** Japan METI v1.2 and beyond, Singapore MAS AI guidelines, EU AI Act all interoperable in practice if not in text. Companies certifiable against multiple regimes with one compliance build.
> - **Medical device approval:** TGA, Medsafe, HSA, PMDA harmonised on Software-as-Medical-Device classification. Cross-recognition pathways operational.
> - **Financial services:** APRA, MAS, JFSA, RBNZ aligned on operational resilience standards. DORA-equivalent provisions adopted bilaterally with the EU.
> - **Critical materials:** Australia, Japan, Singapore, NZ aligned with U.S. and EU export-control regimes. Common provenance and traceability standards.
> - **Sandbox passporting:** active financial-services sandbox-to-sandbox bridges operating between MAS, APRA, JFSA pilot regimes.

## How convergence happens without a treaty

Regulatory convergence in Asia-Pacific has historically failed when attempted through formal multilateral instruments. ASEAN financial integration has moved slowly. APEC harmonisation has been mostly aspirational. The successful convergence pattern is bilateral, sectoral, and incremental.

The mechanism is recognition rather than harmonisation. Singapore's HSA recognises FDA approvals as a basis for expedited review. Australia's TGA accepts certain CE Mark approvals. New Zealand's Medsafe defers to TGA decisions. Japan's PMDA has signed bilateral cooperation agreements with the FDA and EMA. Each is a one-sided recognition that compounds with others until the regional standard exists in operational fact even if not in formal text.

The financial-services parallel is the same. MAS-FCA, MAS-CSSF, and APRA-MAS bilateral financial-services regulatory bridges allow regulated entities in one jurisdiction to apply for licensing in another with reduced friction.[^1] The bilateral count has roughly doubled since 2020. By 2028, on current trajectory, the bilateral network is dense enough to constitute a regional regulatory passport.

## What convergence does to T1 portfolio companies

A T1 company that holds a primary licence in one of the four markets gains a structural pathway into the other three. The pathway is not automatic. It still requires local counsel, local capital, and local entity structuring. But the regulatory submission package, the internal compliance work, and the policy posture transfer with reduced cost.

The implication for valuation is direct. A T1 medtech with TGA approval and FDA 510(k) at Series A is, under convergence, also a credible pathway to HSA, Medsafe, and PMDA. Each additional pathway is an option with quantifiable strike price and time value. Series A valuations under convergence reflect a wider set of options than under the current state.

The expected case is that the option value is mispriced at seed. Seed valuations price on first-licence path. Convergence adds secondary licence optionality without changing the seed price. The arbitrage closes by Series B or C as the secondary regulatory submissions begin to produce evidence.

---

## What convergence does to T2 portfolio companies

A T2 vendor deployed inside one regulated buyer in one market becomes a reference deployment for buyers in adjacent markets, faster than under the current regime. Convergence accelerates the cross-border procurement of compliance technology because the buyers themselves are operating against more similar standards.

The mechanism is observable now. A KYC vendor deployed inside an MAS-licensed bank is being purchased by APRA-regulated banks because the underlying compliance frameworks are increasingly aligned. The same is true for clinical trial software across PMDA, TGA, and Medsafe-regulated trial sponsors. The procurement-reference asset travels.

The valuation implication is similar to T1. Seed-stage T2 vendors price on initial-buyer reference. Convergence expands the addressable buyer set without changing the seed price. The arbitrage closes as the cross-border deployment evidence accumulates.

## Capital and exit implications

Under convergence, exit acquirers expand their geographic interest. North American strategic acquirers have historically preferred APAC targets with U.S. revenue concentration. Convergence reduces the friction in extending APAC operations, which makes pure-APAC targets more interesting to U.S. acquirers. EMEA strategic acquirers, similarly, find regulatory-aligned APAC targets easier to integrate.

The IPO path also widens. Cross-listing between ASX and SGX, between TSE and SGX, and between dual-class structures domiciled in Singapore and the U.S. become more credible because the regulatory baseline is similar. The narrowness of the public exit market in Asia-Pacific has historically been a structural drag on venture returns. Convergence relaxes the drag.

The capital base on the LP side also responds. Sovereign wealth and pension capital from larger jurisdictions allocate more readily to regional funds when the regulatory architecture is recognisable. Australian super funds, Singapore's sovereign vehicles, and Japan's institutional capital can co-deploy with less structuring overhead.

---

## What convergence does not solve

The four-market arithmetic still requires market-specific execution capability. Convergence reduces the friction; it does not eliminate the local relationships, language, and procurement-process competence that drive deal access. A pan-APAC fund that hires only English-speaking sourcing talent in Singapore and assumes Japanese, Australian, and Korean coverage will not benefit from convergence. The structural advantage accrues to funds that have built local capability.

Convergence also does not eliminate the absolute valuation differential between APAC entry and Western exit. The differential narrows as APAC capital deepens, but it does not close inside a five-year window. The seed-stage entry discount remains material in 2028 even under aggressive convergence.

---

## The portfolio construction under path A

Concentrate on T1 with multi-licence optionality and T2 with cross-border procurement reference. Underweight pure-domestic plays. Anticipate an exit acquirer mix weighted to North American strategic and U.S./European IPO. Hold longer than the seven-year venture standard, because the asset compounds during the convergence phase. Reserve dry powder for follow-on into companies that demonstrate cross-market traction by Series A.

Path A is the expected case. The next chapter examines path B, which is plausible enough to require an explicit construction response.
