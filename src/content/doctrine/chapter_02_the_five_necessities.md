**The Necessity Doctrine, Chapter 2**
*2 May 2026*

---

Five categories. Not seven, not three, not whatever number makes a hexagon look balanced. Five domains where capital deployment, regulatory obligation, and geopolitical urgency are converging at the same time, in the same direction, on the same timeline. Every other "regulated sector" is a subset, a synonym, or a distraction.

The categories are food security systems, biomedical systems, power systems, autonomous systems, and critical materials and supply integrity. The remainder of this chapter explains why each cleared the bar and why several adjacent candidates did not.

> **What qualifies a Necessity**
>
> - **Universality:** statutory regulatory architecture in every OECD economy
> - **Acceleration:** measurable increase in regulatory output 2024-2026
> - **Durability:** cross-border governmental coordination, not single-jurisdiction policy
> - **Capital signal:** sustained venture deployment through the 2022-2024 correction
> - **Geopolitical anchor:** treated as critical infrastructure in national security planning

---

## The frame is necessity, not opportunity

Opportunity is the word venture investors use for markets where a better product can win. Necessity is the word governments use for markets where a worse product cannot be allowed to fail. Capital flows to the first frame for fifteen years and then re-prices to the second.

The Five Necessities sit in the second category. Each is a domain where private failure has public consequence, which is why each has accumulated regulatory architecture over decades. That architecture is what creates the barrier the doctrine identifies. The selection criteria are listed in the sidebar above.

Adjacent verticals fail at least one of the criteria. Education technology has regulatory architecture but no geopolitical anchor. Real estate technology has capital signal but minimal cross-border coordination. Retail technology has neither. Most of what venture capital calls "regulated" is actually consumer-protected, which is a different kind of barrier and a thinner one.

## Food security systems

Every OECD government now treats food production, distribution, and traceability as critical infrastructure. The framing shift happened between 2020 and 2024.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration's Final Rule 204 on food traceability becomes enforceable on 20 January 2026, after a one-year delay from the original schedule.[^1] It mandates electronic record-keeping across the supply chain for foods on the FDA's traceability list. Compliance cost estimates from industry trade associations span a wide range, reflecting uncertainty about scope rather than methodology.

The EU's Farm to Fork Strategy and accompanying General Food Law revision pushed mandatory digital traceability onto every food business operator above SME thresholds by 2027.[^2] Japan established a Food Security Strategic Headquarters in 2022 and codified the Food Supply Stability Act in 2024, designating eleven food categories as specified critical materials.[^3]

Capital deployment tracks the regulatory motion. Global agritech and food-tech venture funding ran at multi-billion-dollar levels in 2025, depressed from the 2021 peak but stable through the broader correction.[^4] The compliance software layer inside that figure is growing faster than the application layer above it.

## Biomedical systems

Healthcare is the most regulated sector in every developed economy. The pace of new statutory instruments accelerated through 2024-2025.

The FDA issued final guidance on AI/ML-enabled medical devices in late 2024, formalising the Predetermined Change Control Plan as a pathway to ship updates without re-clearing the device.[^5] Multiple U.S. states passed AI-in-healthcare legislation in 2025. The EU's Medical Device Regulation and In-Vitro Diagnostic Regulation moved into hard enforcement after their multi-year transitional periods.

Japan's PMDA opened a fast-track pathway for AI-enabled diagnostic devices in 2025. Singapore's HSA tightened licensing for medical device software classified above Class A. Australia's TGA introduced a new framework for software-as-medical-device incorporating post-market AI surveillance.[^6]

Biomedical systems carry the highest in-thesis company count in the four-market universe. 1,269 classified companies, 19 T1 regulations and 1 T2 regulation in scope.[^7] The proportion flowing into companies that ship inside a regulatory framework rather than around it has been rising for three consecutive years.

## Power systems

Grid stability, generation, storage, and transmission are now permanent regulatory subjects in every OECD economy. The framing has moved from "energy policy" to "critical infrastructure."

The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism transitional reporting period ended on 31 December 2025; the definitive period and customs declarations for the six covered sectors began 1 January 2026.[^8] The U.S. Inflation Reduction Act has triggered hundreds of billions of dollars in announced capital deployment across covered technologies.[^9] Australia's AEMO published the 2024 Integrated System Plan committing A$122 billion in transmission investment by 2050.

The regulatory architecture is mature. The compliance burden is accelerating. Energy-related venture funding sustained through 2024 and 2025 at levels comparable to financial services, despite the wider venture pullback.

---

## Autonomous systems

Any system that operates physical assets without a human in the loop crosses a regulatory boundary. The list of such systems is growing.

The FAA finalised 14 CFR Part 108 covering routine drone operations beyond visual line of sight in 2025.[^10] Japan's Civil Aeronautics Act amendment extended Level 4 drone authorisation. The IMO published the Maritime Autonomous Surface Ship Code, opening the regulatory pathway for autonomous shipping. NHTSA's Automated Vehicle framework moved through public consultation in late 2025.

The category captures more than vehicles. Autonomous warehouse systems, autonomous mining equipment, autonomous agricultural machinery, and autonomous defence platforms each operate under sector-specific regulatory regimes. Each regime is being rebuilt for AI-driven decision-making rather than rule-based automation.

---

## Critical materials and supply integrity

Lithium, rare earths, semiconductors, and pharmaceuticals are now bracketed under national security export controls in every major economy. The bracketing is statutory and recent.

The U.S. CHIPS and Science Act allocated $52 billion in semiconductor incentives subject to ongoing eligibility audits. The EU's Critical Raw Materials Act came into force in May 2024, designating 17 strategic raw materials and setting 2030 supply targets.[^11] Japan's Economic Security Promotion Act, enforceable from 2023, designates eleven specified critical materials including semiconductors and pharmaceuticals.

Australia released its Critical Minerals Strategy 2023-2030 with A$4 billion in announced facility funding.[^12] The supply integrity layer (track-and-trace, provenance verification, export-control compliance software) is itself becoming a regulated category.

---

## What did not make the cut

Mobility outside the autonomous-systems envelope. Real estate. Education. Retail. Generic enterprise software. Consumer health. Each has regulatory exposure but fails the universality, durability, or geopolitical-anchor test.

This is not a complete map of regulated venture activity. It is the shortlist of categories where the regulatory architecture is deepest, the capital signal is strongest, and the geopolitical commitment is durable enough to sustain a ten-year fund cycle.
