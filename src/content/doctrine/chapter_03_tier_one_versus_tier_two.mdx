**The Necessity Doctrine, Chapter 3**

---

The deal filter is binary. Either the company sits inside the regulatory perimeter, or it sells across it. T1 requires statutory approval to ship. T2 serves buyers who require statutory approval to operate. Everything else is unregulated and falls outside the doctrine.

For T1, the barrier is the licence. For T2, the barrier is the institutional sales motion that grants access to regulated buyers. Both compound. Neither is built quickly.

> **The classifier**
>
> - **T1:** Company is the regulated entity. Licensed lender, payments institution, medical device manufacturer, energy retailer.
> - **T2:** Company sells to regulated entities. KYC software, clinical trial systems, grid management, pharma QA tooling.
> - **Conservative classifier:** assigns T1/T2 only where regulatory exposure is structural, not incidental.
> - **Empirical anchor:** 33.5% of the 19,416 four-market universe passes (6,505 companies). 2,625 are seed-stage in scope.[^1]

---

## Why a binary

Most venture frameworks tier by sector or stage. The doctrine tiers by regulatory posture because that is the variable that predicts barrier strength.

A sector tier tells you whether the company is in fintech. A regulatory posture tier tells you whether it has an FCA licence, an MAS Major Payment Institution licence, or no licence at all. The valuation, sales motion, unit economics, and exit path are different for each.

---

## Tier 1: the licensed entity

T1 companies hold the licence, permit, certification, or approval. They are the entity the regulator inspects.

A digital bank holding an MAS Digital Bank licence. A medical device company with FDA 510(k) clearance. A drone operator with FAA Part 135. A renewable energy retailer with an AEMO market participant authorisation.

The barrier is the licence itself. Licences are slow to issue, expensive to maintain, rare to lose once held. An entity that has cleared a Series A licensing review is materially more likely to clear the next jurisdiction's review than a competitor without prior approvals.

T1 entry valuations carry a measurable premium even at seed. The optionality of the next licence is itself an asset.

---

## Tier 2: the seller across the perimeter

T2 companies serve T1 entities. They ship compliance software, monitoring, audit infrastructure, model risk management, any technical substrate the regulated buyer needs to remain regulated.

KYC and AML platforms sold to banks. Clinical trial management software sold to pharma. Grid telemetry sold to network operators. Pharmaceutical QA tooling sold to drug manufacturers.

The T2 barrier is the sales motion. Institutional procurement, security reviews, regulatory references, audit-ready deployment. Each gets harder to replicate as the buyer's regulatory exposure deepens.

T2 has a structural advantage T1 does not have. The regulatory burden compounds on the customer, not the vendor. As the buyer's obligations grow, the vendor's contract value grows.

---

## What the binary excludes

A SaaS productivity tool sold to a hospital is not T2 unless it differentiates on HIPAA compliance. A payments processor for retail merchants is T1 only if it holds the relevant licence. A telemedicine platform is T1 if it holds the clinical practice authorisation, T2 if it sells to a clinic that does, unregulated if it positions as consumer wellness.

The exclusion is intentional. Companies merely adjacent to regulation compete on distribution, brand, and network effects. That is the older venture playbook.

---

## What changes when the filter is applied

T1 has higher gross margins because the licence creates pricing power. T2 has stickier revenue because compliance switching costs are high. T1 acquirers are strategic incumbents; the price is set by regulatory advantage. T2 acquirers are platform consolidators; the price is set by recurring revenue durability.

A regulated-sector Necessities fund that does not classify by tier is choosing companies that look regulated rather than companies that are regulated.
