# Software Is No Longer Eating the World

**The Necessity Doctrine, Chapter 1**
*1 May 2026*

---

Software is no longer eating the world. Regulation is eating software. The four markets that have built the deepest regulatory architecture are where seed-stage value will be captured this decade.

This is not a contrarian position. It is the observable state of the market, visible in public data that most venture investors have not reconciled with their deployment strategies. The claim is falsifiable. The evidence is timestamped. The implications for fund construction are specific enough to be wrong.

> **Regulatory State of Play**
>
> - **900+** AI-related laws, regulations, and policy instruments enacted globally since 2016 (OECD AI Policy Observatory, 2025)[^1]
> - **156** documented AI enforcement actions globally in 2025, up from 43 in 2024 (Stanford HAI AI Index, 2026)[^2]
> - **$24.3B** global RegTech market in 2025, projected to reach $112B by 2033 at 21.1% CAGR (Grand View Research, 2025)[^3]
> - **59** U.S. federal AI regulations introduced in 2024, more than double the 25 recorded in 2023 (Stanford HAI AI Index, 2025)[^4]
> - **85%** of compliance officers report requirements have become more complex in the past three years (PwC Global Compliance Survey, 2025)[^5]

---

## The original thesis and its shelf life

The Andreessen thesis had a shelf life, and it expired. "Software is eating the world" was a correct observation about markets with low regulatory density. It stopped being predictive when capital shifted to markets where the barriers are statutory, not commercial.

Marc Andreessen published the essay in August 2011. Software did eat retail, media, music, telecommunications, and advertising. Those industries had shallow regulatory architecture. A better product with lower distribution cost won, and software provided both.

Financial services, healthcare, defence, and energy did not get eaten. They got regulated further. The fourteen years since Andreessen's essay have produced more technology-specific legislation than the preceding forty years combined.[^6]

By February 2026, even the original thesis started consuming itself. Morgan Stanley flagged a $1 trillion shock in SaaS valuations as AI displaced the software layer Andreessen championed.[^7] The era is closing.

---

## Regulation is the new barrier builder

Regulation builds barriers that compound. The venture industry spent fifteen years treating it as friction to route around, lobby against, or defer until Series B. That framing was coherent when the addressable markets were advertising and social networking. Those markets had shallow regulatory architecture.

The markets that matter now are different. Financial services generated $52 billion in venture funding in 2025. Healthcare and biotech attracted $71.7 billion.[^8] Together, these two sectors accounted for nearly 30% of the $425 billion deployed globally.

These are not markets where a better product wins on distribution speed. They are markets where a product that cannot demonstrate compliance does not ship.

The EU's Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) became enforceable on 17 January 2025. It covers every financial entity in the European Union. Projected incremental RegTech spending from DORA alone: $3 to $4 billion between 2025 and 2028.[^9]

The EU AI Act began phasing in from February 2025. Penalties for high-risk violations reach €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover.[^10] Japan passed its AI Promotion Act in May 2025.[^11] 47 countries now have active AI legislation.[^12]

This is not a temporary enforcement cycle. This is structural. The regulatory architecture is being built, and it is not coming down.

---

## Where the capital is actually going

The standard venture narrative says capital follows innovation. The data says capital follows regulatory obligation.

Global RegTech spending was $24.3 billion in 2025. It is projected to reach $112 billion by 2033.[^3] That is a 21.1% compound annual growth rate across an eight-year forecast window.

For context, global SaaS revenue grew at roughly 13% CAGR over the same period. Compliance infrastructure is growing faster than the software it is supposed to support.

Nine in ten business leaders expect regulatory demands to increase their operating costs by up to 30%.[^13] The average U.S. business already incurs $10,000 per employee in regulatory compliance costs.[^14]

Four out of five compliance leaders report that limited budgets or headcount have blocked their ability to meet requirements.[^15] The obligation grows. The capacity does not.

The gap between regulatory obligation and organisational capacity to meet it is widening. That gap is the market.

---

## The conservative classifier

A conservative classifier applied to the 2025 global venture dataset produces a clear result. Tier 1 and Tier 2 regulated sectors captured 33.5% of total venture deployment.[^16] Those sectors are financial services, healthcare, defence, and energy/infrastructure.

One third of global venture capital already flows into sectors where regulatory architecture is the primary barrier to entry. Not distribution. Not brand. Not network effects. Statutory compliance.

That 33.5% figure uses a conservative classification. It excludes adjacent regulated verticals: education technology subject to FERPA, property technology subject to local planning codes, food technology subject to FDA and EFSA approval pathways. A broader classifier would push the share above 40%.

The majority of venture dollars still flow into sectors with shallow regulatory architecture. But the share going to deeply regulated markets is rising. The returns profile in those markets is structurally different.

Barriers created by regulation are harder to erode than barriers created by capital or network effects. They compound rather than decay.

---

## What this means for seed

Seed-stage investors face a choice that most have not yet articulated.

You can deploy into markets where the barrier to entry is capital and speed. Those barriers erode as capital becomes abundant and AI compresses development cycles. Or you can deploy into markets where the barrier to entry is regulatory. Those barriers are getting taller every year.

The first path is the venture industry's muscle memory. The second path requires a different toolkit:

- Regulatory mapping
- Compliance architecture assessment
- Jurisdiction-specific go-to-market sequencing
- Portfolio construction that accounts for longer deployment timelines and stickier revenue

This is not a thesis about regulation being good or bad. It is a thesis about where durable value accrues at the earliest stage of company formation. The answer is in the markets where the regulatory architecture is deepest. That architecture converts from obstacle to barrier once a company has cleared it.

The four markets where this dynamic is most pronounced are financial services, healthcare, defence, and energy. Each has built regulatory architecture over decades. Each is now accelerating that build. And each produces a specific pattern at seed stage that the rest of this paper will examine.
