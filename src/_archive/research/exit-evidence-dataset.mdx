The laziest version of this thesis says "APAC sources, Western acquirers exit." That is one exit pathway of three, and it is not even necessarily the dominant one. LPs underwriting this fund should understand that the exit architecture is plural, and that the fund's return distribution does not depend on any single lane.

## The three pathways

| Pathway | Mechanism | Empirical anchor (2010 to 2015 cohort) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Strategic acquisition, NA / EMEA** | Western strategic needs developed-APAC regulatory infrastructure to enter the region. Pays a control premium to buy a company that has already built licences, compliance teams, and regulator relationships. The premium is time, not IP. | Share of the 722 combined strategic-M&A exits in the four-market cohort |
| **2. Local and regional public listings** | ASX, Tokyo Stock Exchange (incl. TSE Growth), Singapore Exchange, NZX. Lower friction for founders: no cross-jurisdictional carve-out, no FX hedging, shareholder base that understands the regulatory context. | 864 public-listing exits across the 19,416 four-market cohort |
| **3. Regional strategic M&A** | Japanese corporates buying AU/SG companies. Australian listed mid-caps rolling up NZ regulatory-infrastructure assets. Singapore-listed conglomerates acquiring ASEAN-facing logistics compliance businesses. Less glamorous than Silicon Valley acquisition. Real, liquid, historically reliable. | Share of the 722 combined strategic-M&A exits |

Combined, the 2010 to 2015 cohort recorded **1,586 completed exits among 19,416 companies, an 8.2% aggregate exit rate over a 10 to 15 year post-founding window**, across all three pathways and all sectors. The fund's underwriting treats this cohort exit rate as the unweighted empirical baseline. The regulated-sector subset performs meaningfully better.

## How the regulatory premium compounds

The exit mechanics are geography-driven, not sector-driven. A health tech company and a critical minerals company in the same market exit through the same channels, face the same buyer dynamics, and benefit from the same structural forces. The regulatory ladder is sector-specific. The exit infrastructure is market-specific.

The filtration is progressive, validated across 62 post-IPO technology companies in the four target markets:

| Stage | Regulated share | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| Seed (baseline) | ~26% of pipeline | Near-parity entry. The regulatory premium is not yet priced. |
| Series B/C | 33% | Regulated companies surviving at a higher rate than unregulated peers. |
| Series D+ | 39% | Unregulated companies being outcompeted at scale. |
| Post-IPO | 42% | 1.4x exit concentration ratio for regulated companies. |

The cumulative funding premium widens from 1.09x at pre-seed to 2.12x at Series D+. Each additional credential multiplies, not adds: a company with TGA plus PMDA plus HSA clearance is worth materially more than 3x, because the acquirer would need to run three independent regulatory processes to replicate it. That replication cost, measured in years and hundreds of millions, is the exit premium.

## Build-vs-buy calculus

For any Tier 1 strategic acquirer entering a regulated market organically, the path from intention to operational compliance runs through three sequential phases:

- **Credential acquisition.** 12 to 36 months per regulated domain, dependent on jurisdiction and pathway complexity.
- **Multi-market establishment.** 2 to 3 markets at minimum to achieve the network effects that make the platform defensible.
- **Compliance team build-out.** $10M to $50M in cumulative regulatory affairs, legal, and government relations staffing, varying by Necessity intensity.

Aggregated across a realistic organic programme, the direct expenditure to replicate a Series B-stage target with embedded regulatory infrastructure is estimated at $40M to $150M before a single recurring revenue dollar is attributable to the regulated capability. Autodesk's acquisition of Payapps (~$390M, 2024) to access AU / NZ construction payment compliance, and Hims & Hers's acquisition of Eucalyptus ($240M cash upfront, up to $1.15B total, 2026) to access telehealth licensing and ACHS accreditation across AU / UK / DE / JP / CA, each imply the acquirer's organic alternative exceeded the acquisition price.

Against an acquisition price of $50M to $300M for a company with that infrastructure already in production and a live customer base, the acquirer's economic case is precise: purchase price is an acceleration fee, not a premium. The implicit denominator in every build-vs-buy analysis is the opportunity cost of delayed market entry. In regulated markets where first-mover licensing advantages compound with three to five year customer switching costs, a 24-month delay forfeits cohort economics that cannot be recovered post-entry regardless of eventual product parity.

## Three structural limits on the exit thesis

**Earlier-cycle Necessities lag the proof set.** Food Security Systems and Critical Materials and Supply Integrity carry limited precedent exits. The regulatory mandates driving them are 2024 to 2028 legislative events. The exit pattern is expected to follow Biomedical Systems and Power Systems with a 2 to 3 year lag.

**Japan exit timelines run longer.** PMDA and MLIT pathways are the most rigorous in the four markets. Japanese portfolio companies should be modelled at 7 to 10 year holding periods, not 5 to 7.

**The thesis requires cross-border M&A.** If Western strategics stop acquiring APAC assets, the primary exit channel is impaired. Domestic and intra-APAC M&A provide a fallback, but at lower multiples. This is a macro scenario, not a sector-specific one. It is the falsification condition the fund explicitly underwrites against.

## What needs to hold

No single exit pathway has to hold. What needs to hold: Five Necessities remain regulated, four markets remain jurisdictionally legible, three-lane exit architecture remains available. Regulators are currently reinforcing all three, not weakening them.
